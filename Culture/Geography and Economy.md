# Geography and Economy

See [[Sisician]] for the hub page and [[Culture/History]] for the timeline this setting supports.

## Why Dalmatian?

Dalmatian is a much better historical source than French for this project. French would create a familiar counterfactual: French-speaking conquerors + Germanic population = England-like result. Dalmatian produces something stranger and more historically grounded: Romance-speaking Adriatic elite + South Slavic population + Byzantine/Orthodox religious world = Sisician.

Dalmatian was a Romance language of the eastern Adriatic, historically associated with cities and islands including Zadar, Split, Dubrovnik, and Krk. It was eventually displaced by Slavic languages and other Romance varieties (especially Venetian) and disappeared as a spoken language. That makes it ideal for conlanging: it provides (1) a genuine Romance ancestor, (2) a geographically plausible contact language, (3) an already historically mixed Balkan/Adriatic environment, (4) enough historical evidence to anchor the design, and (5) enough gaps in the record to permit substantial creative reconstruction.

The key creative move is **not** to invent a Dalmatian-speaking population migrating inland en masse. Instead: **a tiny Dalmatian-speaking noble family/faction establishes itself inland.** That is much more plausible and much more linguistically productive.

## The Geographic Seed

The original geographic question was whether to place the polity in Hungary or Serbia. The conclusion: **western Serbia / eastern Bosnia / the Sava–Drina corridor is the better setting.** Hungary would create a fascinating but much messier language (Slavic + Dalmatian Romance + Hungarian + Latin + German, with complicated Catholic/Orthodox competition). The Sava/Drina region gives a cleaner experiment: South Slavic population + Dalmatian elite + Byzantine/Orthodox influence + Hungarian pressure from the north.

The eventual epicenter became specifically associated with **Siscia / modern Sisak**, because of its Roman history and strategic location. (A modern map search also flagged **Domuskela**, at approximately 44.92, 19.18, as a possible modern geographic epicenter for the fictional cultural area, though the con-history ultimately centers on Siscia/Sisak and the Sava corridor.)

The broader imagined polity: **Adriatic coast → Bosnia → Sava/Drina corridor → Siscia/Srem → Pannonian plain.** The desired linguistic geography is a gradient rather than a clean boundary — Adriatic Dalmatian → Dalmatian-dominant towns → mixed riverine dialects → Slavic-dominant countryside. The middle variety eventually becomes the language.

## Why Siscia?

Siscia (modern Sisak) is an exceptionally useful historical anchor. It lies at the confluence of the Kupa and Sava rivers, with the Sava continuing toward the Danube and the Kupa connecting toward the northwest. The Roman settlement was strategically important as a crossroads of waterways and overland routes, and Roman Siscia developed into a major military and transport center in Pannonia — abundant land, forests, nearby mineral resources, and routes connecting the region with the wider Pannonian network.

This gives the con-history a very useful principle: **the dynasty does not need to invent the strategic importance of the site. It inherits and revives an old one.** The Roman past also gives Sisician culture an unusually strong relationship with ruins, reused earthworks, old roads, Latin inscriptions, and Roman political memory.

## Pre-Founding Roman Siscia

Before the Slavic period, Siscia was a major Roman center, with river access, military importance, proximity to mineral resources, forests and agricultural land, and connections between the Adriatic hinterland, the Pannonian basin, the Danube, and the Balkans. The fictional dynasty therefore does not create a city ex nihilo — it **reoccupies and fortifies a place whose geography has already made it important for centuries.**

This creates an archaeological layering that should become culturally important: prehistoric settlement → Roman Segestica/Siscia → early medieval Slavic region → Sisician dynasty → later medieval polity → modern Sisician cultural memory. Roman ruins can therefore become part of the identity of the people.

By approximately 1000 AD the inland Sava basin is fundamentally Slavic/Croatian-speaking, and Dalmatian Romance is not a major inland vernacular — which is precisely why the founding event must be a small elite arrival rather than a demographic migration (see [[Culture/History]] and [[Culture/Sociolinguistic History]]).

## The Commercial Logic

The dynasty survives because the location is commercially useful. Original formulation: *"Siscia (modern Sisak) sits at the confluence of the Kupa and Sava rivers. That position controls a secondary salt and timber route running from the Adriatic hinterland into the Pannonian plain. The dynasty doesn't need to own salt mines or forests — it just needs to own the crossing, the warehouse, and the chain across the river. Every cargo of salt, timber, hides, and slaves moving through pays a toll."*

The family does not need a huge agricultural domain. It needs a crossing, a ferry, a warehouse, a toll chain, river docks, guards, merchants, storage, and political recognition. Revenue comes from salt, timber, hides, grain, livestock, river cargo, military supplies, slaves (in the historically appropriate early-medieval context), and later manufactured goods. The Sava timber trade is especially useful because timber connects the inland region with Adriatic shipbuilding.

The basic economic principle: **the dynasty owns the bottleneck rather than the resource.** That money pays for walls, soldiers, bribes, diplomacy, marriages, clerks, churches, warehouses, and political patronage.

## The River as Cultural Structure

The Sava should be more than scenery — it is the organizing geographical metaphor of the civilization. It connects the Adriatic hinterland, Bosnia, Siscia, Srem, Pannonia, the Danube, and Byzantine/Balkan routes, carrying timber, salt, grain, hides, people, soldiers, merchants, news, religious ideas, and political power. Sisician identity can develop around the concept: *we are the people who stand between worlds because the river passes through us.* This is not merely political — it should influence poetry, architecture, trade, legal terminology, folklore, saints, military symbolism, geography, and political mythology.

## The Roman Layer

Roman Siscia should remain culturally important. The people know that an ancient imperial city existed here before them. The dynasty deliberately reuses Roman walls, roads, foundations, inscriptions, stones, coins, and old earthworks. This creates a cultural phenomenon in which Latin is simultaneously ancestral, prestigious, foreign, sacred-adjacent, bureaucratic, and archaeological. The Sisicians do not need to believe they are Romans — they can instead have a strong idea that their city has always been a place where civilizations meet.
