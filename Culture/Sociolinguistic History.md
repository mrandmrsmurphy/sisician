# Sociolinguistic History

How the language itself changed, generation by generation, across the timeline in [[Culture/History]]. See [[Sisician]] for the hub page.

## The Founding Asymmetry

By approximately 1000 AD, the inland Sava basin is fundamentally Slavic/Croatian-speaking. Dalmatian Romance is not a major inland vernacular. Therefore the founding event cannot plausibly be "thousands of Dalmatian speakers move inland and replace Croatian." Instead: **a tiny Dalmatian-speaking elite arrives and establishes power over an overwhelmingly Slavic population.** That asymmetry is the engine of the entire language.

## The Sociolinguistic Mechanism

The most important linguistic mechanism is **elite bilingualism followed by language shift** — not conquest → forced language replacement, but:

```text
Dalmatian-speaking elite
          +
South Slavic-speaking population
          ↓
       bilingualism
          ↓
   mixed elite speech
          ↓
   urban/castle vernacular
          ↓
  native bilingual children
          ↓
  Slavic becomes dominant
          ↓
Dalmatian becomes substrate/superstrate
          ↓
      Sisician
```

("Substrate" and "superstrate" should be used carefully — for now, the important thing is that Dalmatian is the prestige source while Slavic supplies the demographic base.) This is much closer to how historical languages actually become strange than a conquest-replacement model would be.

## The 1102 Sociolinguistic Situation

At the point of Koloman's charter (see [[Culture/History]] § 1102), the language is still not "Sisician" in the fully developed sense. There are three main spoken populations: the **Dalmatian-speaking elite** (the founding family and some urban/court descendants), the **South Slavic population** (the overwhelming rural majority), and a **mixed bilingual urban population** (merchants, soldiers, craftsmen, clerks, servants, and children of mixed marriages) — this third group is the linguistic laboratory. The key process: **the Dalmatian elite must learn Slavic, but their Slavic is imperfect.** Their children grow up with Dalmatian from parents and elite household members, and Slavic from soldiers, servants, peasants, and neighbors — becoming native bilinguals. This is the beginning of structural interference.

## Generation-by-Generation Language Shift

**Generation 1 — Dalmatian elite + local Croatian.** The founder and first household speak Dalmatian; the surrounding population speaks South Slavic; the elite learns Slavic. At this point, Slavic remains basically Croatian with Dalmatian loans.

**Generation 2 — Bilingual aristocracy.** Children of the founding household grow up bilingual: Dalmatian with parents and older household members, Slavic with the wider population. Their Slavic begins to acquire Dalmatian phonological habits, Dalmatian vocabulary, Romance idioms, possible Romance-influenced syntax, and code-switching conventions. Their Dalmatian begins to acquire Slavic phonology, Slavic morphology, Slavic vocabulary, and local semantic extensions.

**Generations 3–5 — Castle language.** The castle, market, toll station, monastery, and river port become multilingual, and the language begins to stratify by domain: **native Slavic** for household, agriculture, landscape, basic verbs; **Dalmatian** for commerce, administration, military affairs, architecture, luxury goods; **Church Slavonic/Greek** for ecclesiastical vocabulary; **Hungarian** for political/frontier terminology. This is the stage at which a genuine hybrid begins to emerge.

## The Castle as Sprachbund

The castle is the first major incubator: inside it are the noble household, Slavic soldiers, Dalmatian merchants, local peasants, Latin clerks, Orthodox clergy, visiting Hungarian officials, Byzantine diplomats, craftsmen, and travelers — a miniature Sprachbund. The market outside the castle is the second incubator, the monastery the third, the river port the fourth. Together, castle + market + monastery + river form the core of Sisician civilization.

## The Critical Linguistic Reversal

At the beginning: Dalmatian = high prestige, Slavic = low prestige. Later: Slavic = majority language, Dalmatian = elite heritage language. Finally: Sisician = native identity, Dalmatian = ancestral language. This reversal is what makes the language historically convincing — the founding family does not remain an alien ruling caste forever. It becomes the local nobility. Then it becomes the local people. The language follows.

## Linguistic Stages — Working Periodization

**Old Sisician, c. 1000–1150.** Local South Slavic dominant; substantial Dalmatian bilingualism; founding elite still speaks Dalmatian; Latin charter language; early Byzantine/Church Slavonic influence.

**Middle Sisician, c. 1150–1350.** Bilingualism becomes widespread in the urban elite; Dalmatian lexical penetration becomes extensive; Romance-derived vocabulary acquires Slavic inflection; Byzantine administration adds Greek vocabulary; Hungarian adds political vocabulary; mixed urban dialect stabilizes.

**Early Modern Sisician, c. 1350–1650.** Dalmatian ceases to be a living community language; Sisician becomes the native language; Romance loans become opaque; phonological changes obscure etymology; Cyrillic/Latin orthographic competition can be resolved (see [[Culture/Founding Myth and Naming]] and the orthography design notes below); Orthodox identity becomes fully native. **Same window, the same scribal class also revives the Dalmatian postposed article (el/li/la/le) from written legal/notarial records and begins generalizing it productively onto ordinary nouns** — see [[../Grammar/Definiteness|Grammar/Definiteness]] § Two Article Channels, a conscious elite-driven distancing project of the same kind as § The "Eye-Spelling" Tradition below, not organic drift.

**Modern Sisician, c. 1650–present.** Speakers no longer perceive the Romance vocabulary as foreign; the language is unmistakably Slavic grammatically; historical Romance strata are visible only to comparative linguists; Greek/Church Slavonic vocabulary is recognized as religious or learned; the language develops its own literary standard and national identity. **In the 1800s specifically**, the revived noun-article finally displaces the older Slavic long-form/short-form adjective distinction as the language's primary definiteness marker, after centuries of the two coexisting redundantly — see [[../Grammar/Definiteness|Grammar/Definiteness]] § Coexistence, Then Displacement.

## What the Modern Language Should Feel Like

The desired effect: **at first glance, Romance. After examination, unmistakably Slavic.** A foreigner might see a page full of familiar-looking Romance roots, then notice six or seven noun cases, Slavic agreement, Slavic aspect, Slavic verbal morphology, Slavic-derived function words, palatalization, consonant clusters, and Slavic word formation. The language should create the same cognitive effect English creates for Romance-language speakers: "I recognize so many words!" followed by "Why does none of the grammar behave the way I expect?"

## The Modern Ethnolinguistic Puzzle

A modern historical linguist encountering Sisician should initially have difficulty classifying it. The vocabulary suggests Romance. The grammar says South Slavic. The liturgical vocabulary says Greek/Church Slavonic. The writing system says Orthodox Slavic. The political vocabulary says Hungarian/central European. The archaeology says Roman. The correct explanation is not that the language is a strange Romance language — it is **a South Slavic language transformed by a long-lived Dalmatian Romance elite.** This should be one of the great linguistic surprises of the fictional world.

## Orthographic History (open design area)

The founding family is Latin-literate; the Orthodox Church increasingly uses Church Slavonic and therefore Cyrillic. This creates a natural orthographic conflict: **Latin script = charter / coastal / ancestral prestige** vs. **Cyrillic = Orthodox / Slavic / local vernacular**. A plausible development: **c. 1000–1200** Latin dominates official charters while Church Slavonic/Cyrillic is used for ecclesiastical material; **c. 1200–1400** the vernacular increasingly appears in Cyrillic; **c. 1400 onward** Cyrillic becomes the normal literary script, with Latin surviving in diplomatic correspondence, foreign trade, old family documents, inscriptions, and foreign-language material. Desired visual effect: **the language looks Romance in vocabulary but is written in Cyrillic.**

Because the original Dalmatian elite was Latin-literate, the early vernacular could have awkward, non-transparent conventions — do not make the orthography perfectly transparent. Possible phenomena: old Latin spellings preserved in family names; Cyrillic letters used for Romance sounds; etymological spelling; competing Latin and Cyrillic traditions; old scribal abbreviations; inherited spelling conventions that no longer correspond to pronunciation; hard/soft distinctions represented through historical letter combinations. A later standardization could simplify some of these while retaining enough historical irregularity to make the language feel old.

## Decided (2026-09-20): The "Eye-Spelling" Tradition — Syllabic Consonants as Vulgar Slavic

**One concrete instance of exactly this kind of non-transparent convention, with a real social motive behind it.** Real South Slavic (BCS) unremarkably has syllabic /r/ (and /l/) in ordinary words — *vrt* "garden," *smrt* "death," *prst* "finger," no vowel needed at all. Sisician's own colloquial speech still does this too. But the written standard doesn't: an **overscrupulous class of Dalmatian-philic scribes and grammarians**, most plausibly active during Early Modern Sisician (c. 1350–1650, exactly the period when Dalmatian is dying or already dead as a spoken language — see [[Culture/History]] § 1300), "corrected" these native Slavic words on the page by inserting an epenthetic **a**: *vrt* → **vart**, *smrt* → **smart**. The underlying motive wasn't really etymological or phonological — it was that a bare consonant cluster with no vowel *looked* rustic and unrefined next to the vowel-rich Dalmatian/Romance vocabulary these same scribes idolized (see [[../AIOS/memory/project_prestige_register_principle|project_prestige_register_principle]]). A word that visually resembled Latin/Romance syllable structure looked cultured; a word that didn't looked like something a peasant would say.

**This is the sharper, closer-to-home version of "Sisician thinks of itself as still Dalmatian" than the English/French comparison ever needs to be**, precisely because by the time this correcting tradition took hold, no living Dalmatian community remained anywhere to contradict the self-image (unlike English, which can never fully forget that real French speakers still exist next door) — the scribes were free to reshape the written language toward an idealized, no-longer-checkable Romance-ness.

**The spelling eventually reshaped real pronunciation, not just the page** — a real, well-documented type of sociolinguistic phenomenon (spelling pronunciation, where a prescriptive written convention gets internalized by literate generations and becomes how careful speakers actually talk). The result, still true in Modern Sisician (see [[../Phonology/Modern Inventory|Phonology/Modern Inventory]] § Syllabic Sonorants): casual, fast speech keeps the real bare syllabic consonant; careful, written, or socially self-conscious speech uses the "corrected" epenthetic-vowel form. Using the bare syllabic form in a formal context reads as unrefined — "vulgar Slavic," in exactly the words a Dalmatian-phile scribe of the period might have used. See [[Accent and the Slavic Boogeyman]] for the fuller cultural picture this sits inside.
