# History

The con-history timeline. See [[Sisician]] for the hub page, [[Culture/Geography and Economy]] for the setting, and [[Culture/Sociolinguistic History]] for how the language itself changed generation by generation over this same timeline.

> **Status note:** real historical facts are distinguished from the alternate-history construction where useful, throughout this page.

## The Croatian Succession Crisis, c. 997–1000

This is the real historical opening through which the alternate history enters. King Stjepan Držislav died around 997. His sons were Svetoslav Suronja, Krešimir III, and Gojslav. Svetoslav, the eldest, became king, but his brothers opposed his monopoly of power. Historical sources describe a conflict in which Svetoslav was supported by some Dalmatian cities, while Krešimir and Gojslav gathered support among other Croatian groups. The conflict created an opportunity for outside powers, especially Venice and Bulgaria.

This crisis is the excellent place for the fictional founder to make the wrong political decision.

**The fictional founder**, named 2026-09-25, is **Vitalis Madius de Corinio** (vernacular *Vitale Madi de Karin*) — a Dalmatian noble of Zadar, holding the frontier castrum of Karin (Roman *Corinium*) in Sidraga župa for the Crown, who backs Svetoslav and loses. He is not a great lord: a middling frontier commander (*castellanus*), someone with enough wealth (Zadar urban rents, lay tolls and fisheries on the Karin Sea) to maintain a retinue and enough political importance to become dangerous after losing, but not so powerful that his fall destabilizes the kingdom. See [[Culture/Founding Myth and Naming]] § The Founder for the full name derivation and § The Political Tragedy for how backing Svetoslav plays out against Krešimir/Gojslav's rebellion and the Venetian/Bulgarian intervention this section describes.

Original framing: *"Around 1000 A.D., a Dalmatian noble from Zadar backs the wrong brother in the Croatian succession war. He loses. Rather than stay and be executed or exiled to a monastery, he takes his household, retainers, and whatever wealth he can move, and heads inland up the Sava."* This event must remain small — there is no migration of a people, only a migration of a household and political faction.

## The Founding Retinue

The founding party is linguistically mixed from the beginning: the noble family, Romance-speaking household members, Latin-literate clerks, Dalmatian merchants, Slavic soldiers, local guides, perhaps craftsmen, perhaps a few Italian or German commercial contacts. ("Accompanied by a mixed retinue — Slavic soldiers, local guides, a few merchants.") This matters because language contact begins **inside the founding party itself**: the founder's household speaks Dalmatian, the soldiers and local contacts speak Slavic, and the household becomes bilingual almost immediately.

## The Founding Political Fiction

The founder needs a justification for his new lordship — he cannot simply say "I am leaving Croatia and making my own kingdom." Instead he claims **he is acting in the king's interest**, citing frontier defense against Hungarians, Bulgarians, rival Croatian nobles, uncontrolled river traffic, and local Slavic warlords. He establishes a fortified settlement at or near the old Roman site of Siscia. The political fiction: *"I am not rebelling. I am holding the crossing for the Crown."* This lets the founder acquire legitimacy while effectively creating a hereditary territorial lordship.

## The First Castle

The first fortification is not an enormous stone castle. Around 1000: wooden palisade / motte-and-bailey / reused Roman earthworks. The site controls a crossing, a ferry, river traffic, warehouses, roads, and access to the Kupa and Sava. Stone construction comes later, once the family is wealthy enough from controlling commerce (see [[Culture/Geography and Economy]] § The Commercial Logic). The castle evolves: Roman earthworks → wooden fort → reinforced palisade → stone keep → stone curtain walls → later urban center. This architectural evolution should become part of Sisician historical memory.

## The Five Major Historical Moments

The agreed five-moment backbone of the dynasty's survival:

### 1. c. 1042 — The Byzantine Turn
**Function:** survival, not piety. Byzantine power pushes back into the Balkans; the Siscia lords choose Constantinople and accept the authority of the Patriarch. This produces the extraordinary religious condition of a Latin-rite cultural elite that becomes Byzantine-rite/Orthodox — a "Latin-rite Orthodox enclave," understood as a historical anomaly rather than a standard ecclesiastical category. The founding population remains Dalmatian/Romance-speaking, Latin-literate, and culturally connected to the western Adriatic, but the ruling polity adopts Byzantine Christianity, Constantinopolitan ecclesiastical allegiance, eventually Church Slavonic liturgy, and Greek theological influence. The Great Schism of 1054 becomes less a choice than a confirmation — the dynasty has already chosen its side. Full religious detail: [[Culture/Religion]].

### 2. 1102 — Koloman's Cheap Deal
**Function:** legal autonomy. In real history, Koloman of Hungary was crowned King of Croatia in 1102. In the alternate history, he inherits a frontier he does not want to garrison personally. The Siscia family offers a bargain: they hold the Sava crossing in his name, pay a modest annual tribute, and Hungary leaves their internal affairs alone. Koloman grants them a **charter of autonomy** — the first written confirmation of their special status. Political consequence: Siscia becomes a royal frontier lordship, and legally nobody below the king can touch them. Linguistic consequence: Hungarian becomes a language of royal administration, military frontier terminology, titles, legal vocabulary, and diplomatic correspondence — another layer, displacing neither Slavic nor Dalmatian. Sociolinguistic detail at this stage: [[Culture/Sociolinguistic History]] § The 1102 Sociolinguistic Situation.

### 3. 1167 — Byzantine Zenith and Tax Farm
**Function:** wealth and bureaucracy. Emperor Manuel I Komnenos briefly brings Croatia and Dalmatia into direct Byzantine control. The Siscia lords pivot immediately, accepting a Byzantine **tax-farming contract** for the Sava corridor: collecting imperial customs, maintaining the crossing, recording cargo, policing the route. Officially they are imperial tax collectors; unofficially they skim the revenue. This dramatically increases their wealth and bureaucratic sophistication and reinforces the Greek/Byzantine vocabulary layer (Greek terms for imperial administration, Latin/Dalmatian terms for commerce, Slavic terms for local administration, Hungarian terms where the northern political world requires them). When Manuel dies in 1180 and Byzantine power collapses, the family simply stops remitting — nobody is strong enough to take the infrastructure back. Imperial bureaucracy becomes hereditary local bureaucracy: exactly the sort of historical process that should produce fossilized vocabulary.

### 4. 1242 — The Mongol Catastrophe
**Function:** legitimacy and land. The Mongol invasion of Hungary devastates the Pannonian plain, but the Siscia stone castle survives — the Mongols bypass it because it is not worth the siege. Afterward the dynasty shelters refugees, loans grain, buys abandoned land cheaply, restores river commerce, expands its agricultural holdings, and becomes a regional center of reconstruction. Béla IV grants the dynasty expanded privileges for holding the frontier. Political consequence: the Siscia family emerges from the catastrophe richer and more legitimate. Cultural consequence: the dynasty's identity becomes increasingly associated with "defenders of the frontier" — a major theme for later Sisician historical mythology (see [[Culture/Founding Myth and Naming]]). The language gains another potential vocabulary layer through refugees, Hungarian administration, German craftsmen, military reconstruction, new fortification technology, and land law.

### 5. 1290s — Angevin Succession
**Function:** identity crystallization. By the 1290s the founding Dalmatian dynasty has been transformed — intermarried with Slavic nobility, townspeople, military and merchant families — and the descendants are no longer culturally Dalmatian in the original sense. They are **Sisician**. The Angevin succession crisis in Hungary destabilizes the region; the Siscia lords hedge, supporting local bans, competing claimants, whichever faction best preserves their autonomy. Their hybrid language has become a marker of distinct identity: not quite Dalmatian, not quite Croatian, politically useful as proof that the Sisician polity belongs fully to neither rival camp. This is the point at which language becomes an ethnic/political identity rather than merely a contact phenomenon.

## 1300: The End of the Founding Period

By approximately 1300, the original Dalmatian-speaking household has disappeared as a separate linguistic community. But its language has not disappeared — it has been **absorbed into the Slavic vernacular**. The central historical paradox: **the Dalmatian language dies, but its death creates Sisician.** The descendants of the founding family speak Sisician as their first language, and the surrounding population increasingly does too. Dalmatian survives only as vocabulary, names, old documents, formulas, elite terminology, place names, architectural terms, commercial terminology, and historical memory.

## The Premise, in One Passage

Around 1000 AD, during the Croatian succession crisis following the death of Stjepan Držislav, **Vitalis Madius de Corinio** — a Dalmatian noble of Zadar holding the frontier castrum of Karin for the Crown — backs the losing side. Historically, Svetoslav Suronja was overthrown around 1000 by his brothers Krešimir III and Gojslav; the conflict coincided with Venetian intervention in the Dalmatian cities and Bulgarian military pressure.

In the alternate timeline, the defeated Vitalis refuses either execution or monastic exile. He gathers his household, retainers, clerks, merchants, and Slavic soldiers and retreats inland along the Sava. He claims to be acting in the king's interest, establishes himself at the strategic Roman site of Siscia, reuses its ancient earthworks, controls a ferry/crossing, and begins collecting tolls.

His original language is Dalmatian Romance. His soldiers and neighbors speak South Slavic. His clerks know Latin. His Church is initially connected to the Latin Christian world but gradually turns toward Constantinople. His children become bilingual. His grandchildren speak a mixed urban Slavic vernacular. His descendants eventually speak only that vernacular.

The dynasty survives because it controls a river crossing, possesses independent toll revenue, and can serve as a useful intermediary to larger powers. In 1042 it accepts Byzantine ecclesiastical authority. In 1102 it receives Hungarian recognition and a charter of autonomy. In 1167 it becomes a Byzantine tax-farming intermediary. In 1242 it survives the Mongol catastrophe and gains wealth and legitimacy. In the 1290s it survives another succession crisis by playing both sides.

By 1300 the original Dalmatian language is dead in Siscia. But its vocabulary is everywhere. The people are no longer Dalmatian. They are no longer simply Croatian. They are Sisician.
