# Word Order and Register

Decided 2026-09-19. See [[Design Principles]] and [[Nominal Morphology]] for prior grammar decisions. See [[../Etymology/Semantic Domains|Etymology/Semantic Domains]] for the lexical three/four-layer model this page extends into syntax — **register in Sisician is not just about which words you choose, it's about how you order them.**

## SVO — The "Homey" Baseline

Default, unmarked word order: **Subject-Verb-Object**. Plain, basic, standard — pairs naturally with native Slavic vocabulary (see [[../Words/дом|дом]]). The register of ordinary speech and household life.

**Grounding note:** South Slavic languages are already naturally flexible in word order at the structural level — case marking (not position) carries the grammatical-role load, so multiple orders are always grammatical, differing in topic/focus/pragmatics rather than well-formedness. Sisician's SVO-as-"homey" convention doesn't contradict that; it's a *social/stylistic* value laid on top of that existing structural freedom — SVO is picked out as the plain, unmarked pole of a range that was always available, the same way English speakers can use marked fronting/inversion for rhetorical effect without those orders being "wrong." [[Nominal Morphology]]'s case system (5 living cases) is robust enough to support this freedom without ambiguity.

## Freer Order with Fronting — Erudite, Romance-Coded

Departures from SVO — deliberate fronting for emphasis, style, mood — read as elevated, sophisticated, learned, and pair with Dalmatian-derived vocabulary (see [[../Words/каза|каза]]). This is the register of style and rhetorical flourish: marked syntax signaling the same "erudite" value that Romance-derived words already carry lexically.

## Subject-Peripheral Placement at the Discourse Level — "Very Greek"

Two related patterns, both reading as distinctly Greek-flavored: **(1)** stating the subject once, first, and never repeating it across an extended stretch of subsequent clauses; **(2)** holding the subject back until very last, arriving only after substantial preposed material (a periodic construction). Both connect to the Greek/Church-Slavonic liturgical register (see [[../Words/скинија|скинија]], [[../Words/икос|икос]]) — the same layer that carries theological vocabulary now also carries a distinctive discourse-structuring habit.

**Real grounding:** Classical and Koine Greek rhetorical prose is genuinely known for exactly these two features — the long periodic sentence (extensive preposed subordinate/participial material building up to a delayed main clause) and topic-prominent discourse, where rich verbal agreement morphology lets a subject, once established, go unstated across long stretches without ambiguity. Byzantine liturgical Greek carries both habits forward strongly. This isn't just a stylistic hunch — it's a real, well-documented feature of the register Sisician is borrowing prestige from.

## Summary Table

|---|---|---|
