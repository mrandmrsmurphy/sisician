# Definiteness

Decided 2026-09-19. See [[Adjective Agreement]] for the productive system this page's history feeds into, and [[../Sources/Dalmatian|Sources/Dalmatian]] for the source-language framework this extends. See [[../Sisician|Sisician]] for the hub page.

## Two Systems, One Convergent Story

Sisician's ancestors approached definiteness marking from both sides, using structurally the *same strategy* — fuse a demonstrative/anaphoric pronoun onto the modified word — and arrived at very different outcomes. The Dalmatian side mostly lost its device, fossilizing in a handful of lexical categories. The Slavic side kept its device and it became the productive system in ordinary use. Sisician runs on the Slavic system today; the Dalmatian one survives only as scattered relic material.

## Dalmatian's Postposed Proto-Article — Extension, Not Citation

**Calibration, stated plainly:** real Dalmatian's syntax is essentially undocumented — checked against Dalmatian's own Wikipedia entry (2026-09-19), which covers phonology and dialectal variation but has no grammatical treatment of articles at all, consistent with [[../Sources/Dalmatian|Sources/Dalmatian]]'s own framing that its syntax is a gap in the record, not a documented fact. What follows is a well-motivated creative extension, not a citation.

**The real grounding:** a *postposed* definite article (attached after the noun, not before) is a genuine, well-documented Balkan Sprachbund feature — Romanian's *-ul/-a* (*lup* "wolf" → *lupul* "the wolf"), and the same pattern in Bulgarian, Macedonian, and Albanian, all independently. This is the opposite of Western Romance, which preposes its articles (French *le loup*, Italian *il lupo*). Since Dalmatian sits geographically adjacent to exactly this postposing zone, it's plausible — not attested, but plausible — that Dalmatian was developing (or had) the same postposed pattern, from the same ultimate source as the rest of Romance: Latin **ILLE** ("that"), grammaticalizing from demonstrative to article.

**Decided: Dalmatian had this postposed article, and lost most of it — going further than Romanian did.** Where Romanian kept its article fully productive down to the modern language, Sisician's Dalmatian ancestor's article mostly disappeared, eroding past Romanian's *-ul* all the way down to a bare consonant fossil: **-l**.

## Where -l Survives

Not lost everywhere — fossilized specifically in:

- **High-frequency kinship and body-part nouns**
- **Toponyms and hydronyms**
- **Family names and bynames**
- **Fixed expressions and oaths**

**This is a real, well-attested type of pattern, not an arbitrary list.** Grammatical markers that disappear from the productive system regularly survive precisely in these categories cross-linguistically: high-frequency words (kinship, body parts) resist regular change simply through sheer repetition entrenching an older form; proper nouns (toponyms, family names) are referentially fixed and don't get re-analyzed the way ordinary vocabulary does; and fixed expressions/oaths are memorized as whole chunks, immune to the analogical leveling that reshapes freely-generated speech. The same general principle already showed up in this project's own [[../Grammar/Verbal Categories|aorist/imperfect]] and [[../Grammar/Infinitive Loss and Da-Complementation|infinitive-loss]] work — old material survives longest in marked, memorized, or high-frequency corners of the lexicon.

**Resolved (2026-09-19): a single fossil, -l, regardless of gender — not two parallel fossils.** Real Balkan/Romance postposed articles keep masculine and feminine formally distinct by their vowel (Romanian *-ul* from *ILLUM* vs. *-a* from *ILLA*; Bulgarian's *-ът/-a*, Albanian's own gender-marked article all do the same). But that distinction lived entirely in the vowel — and Sisician's own erosion already goes further than any of those real languages, stripping the vowel away entirely. Once the vowel that was the *only* carrier of the masculine/feminine distinction is gone, there's no phonological material left to keep them apart: **-lu** (masc.) and **-la** (fem.) both reduce to bare **-l** by the same general process, not by a separate leveling decision. This isn't an assumption stacked onto the earlier extension — it's the mechanical consequence of the erosion already decided.

**The rare exception the user flagged is worth keeping precise:** in specific situations it may still be recoverable which gender the fossil descends from — not because the article itself preserves any trace, but because the *lost* vowel could leave an indirect footprint on the preceding root (a vowel-harmony effect, or a palatalization of the adjacent consonant triggered by *-e/-a* before it dropped). That's a real, narrow, secondary channel of evidence, not a second living fossil form.

## The Productive System: Slavic Long-Form/Short-Form Marking

**This is where definiteness actually lives in ordinary Sisician speech** — not the Dalmatian fossil, but [[Adjective Agreement]]'s already-established long-form (definite) vs. short-form (indefinite) adjective distinction. Nothing new to build here; this page exists to explain *why* that system won out and what else definiteness-marking left behind, not to duplicate it.

## Why the Slavic System Won, and Why Definiteness Felt Natural to Keep At All

**The etymological parallel matters here.** [[Adjective Agreement]] already established that the Slavic long form is historically short-form-adjective + suffixed ***-jь*** — the same anaphoric pronoun root behind [[../Words/он|он]]'s own oblique stem, fused onto the adjective to mark definiteness. That's structurally identical to what Dalmatian's postposed article was doing: fusing a demonstrative/anaphoric element (Latin *ILLE*, itself originally "that one there") onto a word to mark that its referent is known/definite. **Bilingual speakers in the founding household and its descendants weren't learning an alien concept when they used the Slavic system — they already had the same underlying strategy in their own Dalmatian speech.** This is a real, well-motivated reason definiteness-marking survived as a robust category into Modern Sisician at all, even though the specific Dalmatian device didn't: the concept was reinforced from both sides, even as one side's implementation fossilized and the other's became universal.

## On "a Complicated Set of Changes by This Point"

The clean story — short-form adjective + *jь* = long-form adjective — is the *origin*, not the current synchronic reality. [[Adjective Agreement]]'s actual endings (*-ога, -ому, -ега, -ему*, etc.) are already the *result* of whatever sound changes and analogical reshaping happened to that original compound over the intervening centuries — they aren't a transparent "adjective + separate pronoun" sequence anymore, and this page doesn't attempt to reconstruct that derivation step-by-step. **Flagging this as a real further task, not a gap being papered over:** a full historical derivation (short-form + *jь* → intermediate stages → the modern endings) could be built out later if wanted, the same way [[../Grammar/Personal Pronouns|Personal Pronouns]]'s ЊЕХ innovation was derived explicitly rather than just asserted — it just hasn't been done yet for the adjective long form.

## Explicitly Not Yet Built

- The actual affected vocabulary — no Sisician kinship term, body-part word, toponym, family name, or fixed oath has been coined yet, so nothing currently in [[../Words|Words/]] needs retroactive *-l*-marking (checked: none of дом/каза/скинија/икос/нув fall into the fossil categories).
- The step-by-step historical derivation from short-form+*jь* to the modern long-form endings (see above).
- The rare secondary-evidence channel (root vowel-harmony/palatalization traces of a lost *-e* vs. *-a*) hasn't been worked out for any actual word, since none exists yet in the fossil categories.
