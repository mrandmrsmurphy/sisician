# он (on)

**Status: exploratory/tentative.** Fourth individual pronoun entry, following [[јаз]] and [[ти]] in [[../Grammar/Personal Pronouns|Grammar/Personal Pronouns]]'s paradigm. See [[../Sisician|Sisician]] for the hub page.

## Meaning — Where the Neuter Merger Actually Lands

"He" — but since [[../Grammar/Nominal Morphology|Nominal Morphology]]'s neuter merger, **он also covers every old-neuter-singular referent**: any formerly-neuter noun now takes masculine agreement in the singular, and its pronoun is он, not a separate "it." This isn't a side effect worth a footnote — **this word is the concrete site where that whole merger cashes out.** [[../Grammar/Personal Pronouns|Personal Pronouns]]'s recent § Why Genitive and Accusative Differentiate works out the consequence in detail: the real Croatian on/ona/**ono** nominative contrast used to let speakers distinguish "person" from "thing" at least once, at first mention; Sisician's merger removes that distinction everywhere, permanently, and он is where "he" and "it" now collapse into one word with no formal way to tell them apart from the pronoun alone.

## Etymology — Two Different Roots, Joined by a Documented Mechanism

**This suppletion isn't just "different historical stems, reason unclear," the way [[јаз]]/мене or [[ти]]/тебе are** (both real, both old, neither with a specific documented *mechanism* connecting nominative to oblique). он's split has an actual attested phonological cause.

**Nominative он** ← Proto-Slavic **\*onъ** "he, that (over there)" ← Proto-Balto-Slavic **\*anas** ← PIE **\*h₂énos**, a demonstrative. Real Slavic replaced an *older* anaphoric pronoun, ***jь***, with this demonstrative in the nominative — and per Wiktionary's reconstruction page, "the nominative forms of this pronoun had mostly fallen out of use, and were supplanted by reflexes of *onъ... even in Old Church Slavonic," meaning the replacement happened within Proto-Slavic itself, before any of the daughter languages split off.

**Oblique stem њега/њему/њим** ← that older pronoun, Proto-Slavic ***jь*** ← Proto-Balto-Slavic **\*is** ← PIE **\*ís**/**\*Hyós** (cognate with Latin *is*, Lithuanian *jis*). It survived — but only in the oblique cases, and only with an extra **њ-** it didn't originally have. The reason is a real, specific, documented sound change: **"following a preposition, a prothetic n- is attached to the pronoun in many Slavic languages, including Old Church Slavonic. This probably arose through resegmentation of prepositions that originally ended in -n; through the law of open syllables, it became preferable to consider the final consonant as part of the next syllable, so it was shifted onto the pronoun."** In other words: a phrase like "*vъn jego*" ("in him," with a preposition ending in *-n*) got re-syllabified as "*vъ njego*," and speakers reanalyzed the *n-* as belonging to the pronoun itself. That reanalyzed *nj-* form then generalized to every oblique context, even without a preposition — which is why њега/њему/њим all start with њ- while the nominative он doesn't.

**No jaz-style tension found here.** Checked the comparative picture the same way as [[јаз]] and [[ти]]: South Slavic is stable on the nominative — Serbo-Croatian *ȏn*, Slovene *ȍn* — no branch-conditioned split the way 1st singular *az/ja* had. (East Slavic shows more surface variation — Russian *он*, Ukrainian *він* — but that's a separate, later Ukrainian-specific development, not a South Slavic concern.)

## Register

Same closed-class status as [[јаз]]/[[ти]] — no Slavic/Dalmatian/Greek stratification applies. Per [[../Grammar/Verbal Agreement|Verbal Agreement]]'s pro-drop, он can be omitted once a referent is established, but 3rd-person reference genuinely needs restating more often than 1st/2nd in ordinary discourse — someone else's identity isn't recoverable from verb agreement alone the way "I"/"you" already is from person marking. The T–V distinction ([[../Culture/Formal Address (ти vs ви)|Culture/Formal Address]]) doesn't apply to third person at all — that's a 2nd-person-only system.
